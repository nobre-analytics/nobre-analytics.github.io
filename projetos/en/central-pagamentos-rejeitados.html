The problem

The same rejected payment could go different ways, depending on who looked at it.

When a payment is rejected, someone has to decide what to do: issue a new payment slip, redo the operation, refund the customer or investigate. That means comparing the payment gateway statement, the collections system and the status of the agreement with the creditor.

Rejections were copied by hand before any analysis. There were no written rules, so two people could handle the same case differently. Many cases turned into tickets, and the history of each case was scattered outside the workflow.

The hard part is precedence: the same payment can look like a case for a new slip or for a refund, depending on the status of the agreement.

My contribution

I turned knowledge that lived in people’s heads into explicit rules.

What I did myself
  • Mapped patterns across cases and the precedence between rules.
  • Structured the decision tree and refined it from July to October 2026, as new cases appeared.
  • Built the extraction of rejections, the decision rules and the panel the team works in.
  • Decided that cases without enough evidence go to review instead of receiving a destination.
With other people
  • Gathered and validated the rules with the people who handled payments every day.
External dependencies
  • Refunds, payoffs and proposals are carried out by the people responsible.
  • The data comes from the payment gateway, the collections system and the creditor.

How it works

Suggesting the next step is not the same as carrying out the action.

The rules started with four main categories: issue, redo, refund and review. Later they were split into more specific destinations whenever a real case did not fit. Having a category does not mean the final action is automatic.

  1. Extraction. The day’s rejections are read automatically, with no manual copying. automated
  2. Cross-check. Each case is compared with the gateway statement and the collections system. automated
  3. Next step. The rules suggest issue, redo, refund or review, and record the reason. automated
  4. Action. For some destinations, such as redo, a bot carries out and logs the operation. For the rest, the person responsible does it. automated and person
  5. Review. When the evidence is not enough, the case goes to a person, with no guesswork. person

A second payment for the same instalment is sent to the duplicate payment control (case 03).

What changed

Case handling moved to one place, with the customer’s context.

Analysis and team oversight now happen in the panel, which shows the customer’s other payments in the same case. Every decision has its reason recorded, so someone else can understand why a case went where it did.

Along the way, I investigated a recurring pattern of tickets and found the rule behind it, linked to the normal posting time for payments. I shared that rule with the operation to avoid rework.

How the result was assessedQualitative result

The result is qualitative: written rules, a recorded reason and handling in one place. Human time and rule accuracy have not been formally measured.

There are execution records for the analysis step, but the counting method has not yet been checked for publication. That is why this case shows no run times, success rates or counts.

Limits

What the rules do not do.

  • There is no formally validated accuracy rate.
  • Destinations that depend on a person are still manual, such as refunds, payoffs and proposals. human step
  • Cases without enough evidence get no destination: they go to human review. human step
  • The rules do not pay refunds. Paying the customer stays with the person responsible.
  • The time the system records covers the analysis step, not the full payment cycle or the time to resolve the case for the customer.

Technical details

Simple tools, well-defined rules.

Tools, terms and implementation decisionsFor readers who want the detail

Rules engine: a set of rules written in code that always apply the same decision to the same case and record the reason. Here it suggests the next step; it does not carry out every action on its own.

Phases: first, automatic extraction of rejections; then rules with four categories; from July to October 2026, more specific destinations.
