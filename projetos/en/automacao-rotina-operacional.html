Process automation

A morning routine that starts on its own, before the team arrives

Paulo reorganised the daily preparation of the collections operation. Instead of depending on a person triggering each step, the sequence starts at 6:00, runs in a chain and only calls someone when something fails.

The problem

Preparation finished close to 8:00, and any wait pushed the time back.

The collections team starts at 8:00 and needs the dialer list ready for it. Before, every preparation step depended on a manual trigger: someone checked that the previous day’s tasks had finished, triggered the portfolio distribution to the agencies, waited for it to end and only then triggered the list.

The routine competed for attention with other work in a lean team. In the 29 days observed between Apr 27 and May 30, 2026, the list was ready after 8:00 on 3 days, and on 5 others there were less than 15 minutes to spare.

Paulo’s role

He measured where time was lost before changing anything.

The diagnosis showed the tool was not the bottleneck: time was lost in the wait between one step and the next. So the solution was to chain the tasks and schedule the start, rather than try to speed up each one.

What he led
  • Mapped the steps, timings and dependencies between tasks.
  • Pulled the execution records and measured the wait between the distribution and the list.
  • Designed and validated the chaining and the schedule.
  • Defined failure handling: a new run and, if that does not fix it, a person takes over.
  • Followed the runs after the change.
With the teams
  • The vendor’s support team guided the use of the platform’s chaining feature.
Out of scope
  • The scheduling platform and its code belong to the vendor.
  • Daily monitoring stays with the BackOffice team.

How it works

A scheduled start, steps in sequence and a person only when needed.

  1. Scheduled start. The sequence starts at 6:00, without anyone needing to be online to trigger it. automated
  2. Portfolio distribution. The day’s portfolio is sent to the collection agencies. automated
  3. Dialer list. Starts as soon as the distribution ends, with no trigger to wait for. automated
  4. Monitoring. A person checks the result. If a step fails, it runs again; if it keeps failing, the person takes over. person

6:00 is when the scheduled sequence starts. It is not the time anyone starts work.

Result

The list is now ready before 7:00, and the wait between steps dropped to minutes.

In the three detailed records after the change, the dialer list was ready between 06:22 and 06:57, and the handoff between the distribution and the list took 2.2 minutes at most. Before, that wait reached 41 minutes.

Product decisions

Automate the chain, keep a person in control.

  • Use the feature the platform already had, with no new development and no extra cost.
  • Cap the automatic retries: after the cap, a person takes over, so a recurring failure is never hidden. human step
  • Measure before and after with execution records, not with the team’s perception.
  • Next step: widen the post-change sample and extend the chain to the routine’s digital steps.

Technical details

A process change using a feature that already existed.

Technologies, terms and implementation decisionsFor those who want to go deeper

Task chaining: each task starts when the previous one ends, with no manual trigger. The feature belongs to the vendor’s platform; the work was designing the sequence, the dependencies and the failure behaviour.

Measurement: timings taken from each task’s execution records. The wait was calculated between the end of the distribution and the start of the list.
