Process improvement and automation

How the morning routine stopped depending on manual triggers

Preparing the collections operation depended on a person starting each step, one after the other. I reorganised the steps into a sequence that starts on its own at 6:00 and checked the result in the execution records.

Problem
The dialer list has to be ready before 8:00, when the team starts calling. Each step only began when someone triggered it after the previous one.
My part
I mapped the flow, measured the waits and set up the sequence on the existing platform, with guidance from the vendor’s support team.
What changed
The sequence now starts on a schedule at 6:00, the steps run in a chain, and a person monitors the result and any failures.
Evidence
Before: a 07:30 median over 29 days. After: 3 detailed records, between 06:22 and 06:57.
Measured from records · different sample sizes

The problem

Preparation finished close to 8:00, and any wait pushed it later.

The collections team starts at 8:00 and needs the dialer list ready by then. Before the change, every preparation step depended on a manual trigger: someone checked that the previous day’s tasks had finished, started the portfolio distribution to the collection agencies, waited for it to end and only then started the dialer list.

The routine was one of many tasks for a small BackOffice team, with nobody dedicated to it. If the person was busy when a step finished, the next one sat idle. Over the 29 days observed between April 27 and May 30, 2026, the list was ready after 8:00 on 3 days, and on 5 days there were less than 15 minutes to spare before 8:00.

Before: manual triggers

  1. A person checks that the previous steps have finished
  2. The person starts the portfolio distribution
  3. The next step waits until someone sees the distribution has ended
  4. The person starts the dialer list

After: scheduled sequence

  1. The sequence starts on its own at 6:00
  2. The portfolio distribution runs
  3. The dialer list starts as soon as the distribution ends
  4. A person checks the result and handles failures

My contribution

I measured where the time was lost before changing the routine.

The tool was not the problem. Time was lost in the wait between one step and the next, while nobody triggered it. So the change was to chain the tasks and schedule the start, rather than try to make each task faster.

What I did myself

  • Mapped the steps, timings and dependencies between tasks.
  • Pulled the execution records and measured the wait between the distribution and the list.
  • Set up and validated the task chain and the schedule.
  • Defined what happens when a step fails: it runs again and, if that does not work, a person takes over.
  • Followed the executions after the change.

With other people

  • The vendor’s support team advised on using the platform’s task-chaining feature.

External dependencies

  • The scheduling platform and its code belong to the vendor.
  • The BackOffice team is still responsible for monitoring the routine.

How it works

A scheduled start, steps in sequence and a person monitoring.

  1. Scheduled start

    The sequence starts at 6:00, without anyone needing to be online to trigger it.

    automated

  2. Portfolio distribution

    The day’s portfolio is sent to the collection agencies.

    automated

  3. Dialer list

    Starts as soon as the distribution ends, with no trigger to wait for.

    automated

  4. Monitoring

    A person checks the result. If a step fails, it runs again; if it keeps failing, the person takes over.

    person

6:00 is when the scheduled sequence starts. It is not the time anyone starts work.

What changed

The sequence now starts on its own, and the wait between two steps dropped in the available records.

In the three detailed records after the change, the dialer list was ready before 7:00, and the handoff between the distribution and the list took 2.2 minutes at most.

How to read these numbers.
  • The samples differ: 29 days before and only 3 detailed records after. The three records show the direction of the change, but they are not enough to claim a new average time.
  • The times are when the dialer list finished, not the whole routine. On August 24, a digital step finished at 08:11 and the main task was still running after 08:12. In the other two records, the main task finished at 07:00 and 06:36.
  • A shorter wait between two steps does not prove an equivalent reduction in total working time. Hours of human work were not measured.

Source: the routine’s execution records, compiled by the author.

Limits

What still depends on people and what has not been measured yet.

  • A person still monitors the routine and handles failures that a rerun does not fix.human step
  • The comparison after the change has only three detailed records.
  • The routine has other steps besides the dialer list, and they do not always finish before 8:00.
  • The gain in working hours was not measured.
  • Email alerts were used in the past, but they are switched off in the latest configuration.
  • The exact date the change went live is not recorded here.

Technical details

A process change using a feature the platform already had.

Tools, terms and implementation decisionsFor readers who want the detail

Task chaining: each task starts automatically when the previous one finishes, with no manual trigger in between. It is a feature of the vendor’s scheduling platform; my work was to set up the sequence, the dependencies and the behaviour when a step fails.

Measurement: the times come from each task’s execution records. The wait was calculated from the end of the distribution to the start of the list.
