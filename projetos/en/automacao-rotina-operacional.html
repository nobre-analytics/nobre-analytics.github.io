Morning Operations Routine Automation

The routine that gets the collections operation ready depended on manual triggers, one step after another. I reorganized the steps into a scheduled sequence, and the dialer list was ready earlier on the documented days.

Business problem

Operations started at 8:00, but the preparation depended on someone triggering each step.

The dialer list has to be ready for the collections team to start the day. Because each task only started when a person triggered the previous one, any wait or failure pushed the list close to 8:00 and, on some days, past it.

Operating context

Five steps in sequence, each triggered by hand.

  1. Check that previous tasks and write-offs had finished.
  2. Trigger the portfolio distribution to the collection agencies.
  3. Wait for the distribution to finish.
  4. Trigger the dialer list preparation.
  5. Handle failures and rerun steps when needed.

Three people shared the BackOffice work, and none of them worked only on this routine.

My responsibility

I mapped the flow and set up the chaining, with vendor guidance.

  • I mapped the steps, timings and dependencies between tasks.
  • I identified the manual waits between one step and the next.
  • I set up and validated the chaining and scheduling on the existing platform, with guidance from the vendor’s support team.
  • I defined the failure and rerun path: repeat the step and, if that does not fix it, hand it to a person.

Who did what. The platform and its code belong to the vendor, and their support team advised on how to use the chaining. The mapping, the sequence setup, the validation of the runs and the follow-up on the change were mine.

Analysis and decisions

The bottleneck was the wait between steps, not how long each step took.

In the April and May 2026 records, the wait between the distribution and the dialer list trigger reached 41 minutes on one day. So the decision was to chain the dependent tasks and start the sequence on a schedule, at 6:00, instead of speeding up each task.

The failure path was explicit from the start: a failed step is rerun and, if the problem continues, a person takes over.

How the solution works

A scheduled start, steps in sequence, and a person monitoring the result.

Scheduled startThe sequence starts at 6:00, without someone needing to be online to trigger it.
DistributionThe portfolio is distributed to the collection agencies.
Dialer listStarts when the distribution ends, with no manual trigger to wait for.
MonitoringA person checks the result and handles failures and reruns.

Result and evidence

The dialer list was ready earlier on the documented days.

Band = minimum and maximum of the 29 earlier days; line = median; dots = the 3 documented days after the change.

The scheduled sequence reduced the dependency on manual triggers and made the dialer list available earlier on the documented days.

How it was measured. Historical run records of the routine. Before: 29 days observed in April and May 2026, with a median of 7:30, a minimum of 7:10 and a maximum of 8:02; on 3 of those days the list was ready after 8:00. After: 3 days with detailed records (6:57, 6:38 and 6:22). The times are dialer list completion times, not the end of every task in the routine.

Limitations

What the data does not show yet.

  • Three observations after the change are not enough to draw a conclusion about every day.
  • The times measure the dialer list. The full routine has other steps; in one record, the digital step finished after 8:00.
  • Human effort was not measured in hours before and after.
  • Email alerts were used in the past; the most recent configuration reviewed shows the feature is turned off.

Sustainability

The routine no longer depends on someone triggering each step, but it is still monitored.

The team now has two people, and one of them monitors the morning routine. The team got smaller because a colleague left, not because of the automation. When a step fails, it is rerun; if that does not fix it, a person takes over.

Leadership Principles

How this case connects to Amazon’s principles.

  • Dive DeepI gathered the real dialer list times and the waits between steps before proposing the change.
  • Invent and SimplifyI used the chaining in the existing platform instead of looking for a new tool.
  • OwnershipI validated the runs after the change and made it clear what happens when a step fails.
